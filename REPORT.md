# Quant Network: public-source due diligence

14 October 2026. This note covers Quant Network Ltd, the QNT ERC-20, the public Besu and Fabric code those programmes have cited, and the public record of Project Rosalind, the UK Regulated Liability Network experimentation, the Bank of England Synchronisation Lab, and The Clearing House On-Chain Money Initiative. Vendor pages are treated as claims. Every figure below was read from a contract, a chain call, a registry, or an institutional document. The Overledger developer portal was not probed.

Chain reads for the crowdsale were taken at Ethereum block 26,193,060 via `https://eth.drpc.org`. Holder balances and the spot price were taken the same day from the Ethplorer token API.

## Demonstrated

One externally owned account can still mint QNT. The token at `0x4a220E6096B25EADb88358cb44068A3248254675` is the verified contract `StandardToken`, compiler 0.4.21, with source on Etherscan and a Sourcify partial match. Its `totalSupply()` returns a constant, 45,467,000 QNT. It does not increase that constant when tokens are created. The only privileged function is `mint(address,uint256)`, and the modifier `onlyCrowdsale` allows that call from exactly one address, written in the constructor and never updated: the crowdsale at `0x398e41Ac3d5972b4bAC2320CD130C7A25ca446F7`. There is no function that changes the crowdsale address.

That crowdsale is also verified. Its `owner` is `0xF5E38bBEDC78EfeA055e0C56035AdB320E64C4Bc`, the same account that deployed the token. The account has no contract code, so it is a single key, not a multisig. The crowdsale sets `owner` once in the constructor and has no function that rotates it. At block 26,193,060 the key had nonce 817 and a balance of about 0.38 ETH. `token()` on the crowdsale still points at the QNT contract. `inSale` and `inPresale` are both false, so the public purchase functions revert today. `enableSale()` sets `inSale` back to true with no further check, and `recordPresalePurchase` has no presale-open check at all. `transferTokens()` is `onlyOwner` and is what actually calls `mint`.

The remaining capacity is exact. Presale sales are capped at 15,000,000 QNT and 8,533,112.493976223 QNT have been recorded, leaving 6,466,887.493976223 QNT. The owner can assign that remainder to any address with `recordPresalePurchase` and then mint it, without sending ETH. Regular sales are capped, after `endPresale()` folded the unused presale allowance into the regular cap, at 22,466,887.493976223 QNT, of which 1,431,146.525791589 QNT have been recorded, leaving 21,035,740.968184634 QNT. Minting that second tranche requires `enableSale()` and a purchase at the on-chain rate of 430 QNT per ETH. The ether is forwarded to the owner inside the purchase, so the key must briefly hold about 48,920 ETH and receives it back in the same transaction. It does not have to spend it. The two remainders together are 27,502,628.462160857 QNT. At the Ethplorer price of $202.70 retrieved with the holder snapshot, that is about $5.57 billion of new balances, of which about $1.31 billion can be minted with no ether float. `totalSupply()` would continue to report 45,467,000. This is a supply-integrity fact about QNT. It does not, by itself, move funds inside a central-bank ledger.

The same contract cannot seize, freeze, or rewrite existing balances. There is no owner, no pause, no blacklist, no upgrade proxy, and no burn. `transfer` and `transferFrom` move only the caller's own balance or an allowance the holder has set. The largest balance on the holder list, 9,550,887.900265649 QNT, 21.01 percent of the reported supply, sits on the token contract itself. Nothing in the source spends `balances` of the contract's own address, so that quantity is stuck. It is not a treasury an administrator can withdraw. Across 184,028 holder addresses reported by Ethplorer, the next nine addresses hold 3,559,344.37 QNT, 7.83 percent. The largest of them, `0x826678ad6b1aaa5e1f20e079333d9b66ab22d78b`, holds 600,000.00001 QNT, 1.32 percent, and has no contract code. `0xf977814e90da44bfa03b6295a0616a897441acec` holds 553,381.876 QNT, 1.22 percent, also an externally owned account, and public holder lists label it Binance 8. No threshold, signer set, or foundation multisig is encoded in the token. The confiscation path a pause-and-blacklist token would give an issuer is absent from this bytecode. The live administrative power is inflation by the one crowdsale key.

Besu QBFT, the consensus family named for Rosalind's account-based ledger, finalises a block only after a supermajority of validators sign it. `BftHelpers.calculateRequiredValidatorQuorum` returns `ceil(2N/3)`, and the Besu QBFT documentation states that a block is inserted only once at least two-thirds of validators have signed, and that validators take turns proposing. The same documentation states that if more than one-third of validators stop participating, the network stops creating blocks. A partition that cannot assemble `ceil(2N/3)` signatures therefore cannot finalise, and two partitions cannot both hold that quorum. Membership changes are validator votes, not an external admin key inside Besu itself: `qbft_proposeValidatorVote` is published by existing validators, and the documentation states that a proposal is applied once more than 50 percent of them have published a matching vote. Pending votes are discarded at the epoch boundary, default 30,000 blocks, and each validator re-adds its own outstanding proposal the next time it proposes. A second mode, `validatorcontractaddress`, takes the validator list from a contract. In that mode the keys that can mutate the contract are the keys that can change who finalises blocks. Neither mode says anything about who runs a Quant network. No public artifact lists the validators, the epoch, or the contract behind any Quant deployment. The code does say what a small or commonly controlled set would mean: with four validators, one non-signing validator is enough to stall finality, and three signers are enough to finalise. Round-robin proposal makes the next proposer a function of the validator list and the round, so loss of the current proposer costs a round, and loss of a quorum costs finality.

Fabric's Raft ordering, the family that predates the optional Byzantine orderer, tolerates crashes and does not tolerate a malicious orderer. The current Fabric ordering-service document describes Raft as crash-fault tolerant: a majority of consenters must remain, so three nodes tolerate one crash and five tolerate two. The same document describes the SmartBFT orderer, new in Fabric 3.0, as the path that still commits a single chain when fewer than one-third of orderers are malicious. It draws the inclusion distinction in terms a settlement panel can use directly. Under Raft, submitting a transaction, including submitting it to every orderer, does not guarantee inclusion, because a leader can fail to carry it into a block. Under the BFT orderer, followers that have seen the transaction force inclusion or a leader change. Rosalind's token-based ledger was Fabric, and the final report is dated 16 June 2023, before that BFT orderer existed in a Fabric release. A deployment that is still Raft inherits the crash-only model. A deployment that has moved to SmartBFT has not been identified in any public Quant or central-bank artifact.

Project Rosalind, in the BIS Innovation Hub report of 16 June 2023, was a prototype API layer: 33 endpoints, tested through more than 30 use cases, then closed. The central-bank ledger was simulated twice. The account-based simulation used Hyperledger Besu and a proxy so the contracts could be upgraded. The token-based simulation used Hyperledger Fabric and a contract written to mimic a UTXO model. Overledger was the ledger API used to stand those simulations up quickly. The report states that exploration of ledger and ledger-API choices was limited. It does not record a production ledger selection. It also records a control that was switched off: write calls were designed to carry a JSON Web Signature in `x-jws-signature`, and that check was disabled during the project to speed use-case work, with the report itself calling the signature a production requirement. Personal data was specified to stay at the payment-interface provider, with the API seeing ciphertext. That is a prototype privacy design, published as such. The proxy admin, the Fabric consortium, the contract source, and any later production choice are not in the report.

The later public programmes stay in the same category of evidence. UK Finance's RLN experimentation technical report, published with the summary report on 17 September 2024, describes an experimentation platform. The tokenisation platform was Corda. The report states that Quant Overledger was the component used to connect to that Corda platform, and it names a Quant connector framework against Rosalind, a simulated RTGS, and Open Banking. The workflow engine was Camunda. The report studies atomic settlement and synchronised settlement, including timelocks, as experimental behaviour across partitions. It does not publish a production finality proof or a custody boundary. The Bank of England Synchronisation Lab page lists Quant for "liquidity optimisation for non-financial corporates," using Quant Flow against a simulated RT2. The same page states that the Lab is not a regulatory sandbox and will not support real-money payments. The Clearing House's 5 June 2026 announcement of the On-Chain Money Initiative describes a bank-operated network for tokenised commercial-bank deposits, connected to RTP and CHIPS, and does not name a vendor. Its 24 September 2026 announcement names Quant for the interoperability, orchestration, and transaction-management layer, and says the network is expected to be available to participants in the first half of 2027.

## Claimed only

Quant's own QuantNet pages say the network orchestrates settlement without holding funds, that banks keep their own tokenised-deposit wallets, and that the layer coordinates atomic delivery-versus-payment across ledgers without taking possession. Those sentences are the whole public case for the custody boundary. The UK Finance technical report shows an experimentation orchestrator that coordinates earmarks, locks, and timelocks across ledgers, including a path from a tokenised deposit to the Rosalind prototype, and it treats atomic cross-partition settlement as something the network-of-networks model made harder rather than as a property that had been proved for production. The Clearing House has not published the custody, pause, or failure model of the 2027 network. Until a contract, a scheme rule, or an independent report shows that the orchestrator has no path to debit, delay, or redirect value, "never holds funds" remains a vendor description.

Overledger Authorise is described on Quant's site as central-bank-grade key management and transaction signing, configurable to an institution's own rules, and as having been used in Rosalind. The February 2024 Rosalind case study calls the Rosalind deployment a beta. The BIS Rosalind report describes API authentication and then records that transaction signatures were turned off for the build; it does not mention Authorise. No source, no policy engine, and no description of who populates an allowlist, whether that list can be empty, or whether a role can be self-granted is public. The signing boundary a central bank would need to inspect, hardware module against gateway against application, is not an artifact yet.

Quant's QuantNet launch text says major UK banks are already using the technology in live environments. The Clearing House's 24 September 2026 release says Quant has delivered on-chain capabilities in production and at scale. The documents that can be read in full show a 2023 API prototype, a 2024 experimentation phase on Corda plus an orchestrator, a Bank of England lab that uses no real money, and a US network whose operator says availability is the first half of 2027. Participation in those exercises is real and is cited above. A production settlement system, with a named ledger, a validator or notary set, and a finality rule, is not what those documents contain.

## Absent

- Source for the Rosalind central-bank contracts, including the Besu proxy admin and the Fabric UTXO contract. The BIS report describes both and publishes neither.
- Any public choice of one Rosalind ledger for a later production system.
- Lock-record cleanup inside a payment transaction. UK Finance names a lock-state component in the experimentation stack and does not publish that logic.
- Hash-timelock parameters, timeout values, and the refund path for any QuantNet or RLN flow.
- The validator set, epoch length, and validator-contract admin for any Quant-operated Besu network, and the consenter set and Raft-or-SmartBFT choice for any Quant-operated Fabric network.
- A pause, upgrade, or blacklist authority on any settlement contract other than the QNT crowdsale key documented above.
- Who is on an "authorised parties" allowlist, who adds them, and whether the list can be empty.
- Overledger gateway authentication, rate limits, error behaviour, and what the gateway logs. The developer portal was not tested.
- A multisig threshold or signer identity for any QNT treasury. The largest balances checked are a stuck contract balance and externally owned accounts.
- A burn that reduced QNT supply. The token has no burn function. Supply fell below the 45,467,000 constant only in the sense that unsold capacity was left unminted, and that capacity is still callable as calculated above.
- Beneficiaries of the Goulding Trust, and any group structure beyond Quant Network Ltd and Quant Group Corporation Ltd.
- An insolvency filing. None appears on the two Companies House records read here. Separately, both records show zero registered charges.
- A Digital Securities Sandbox participation record for Quant. On the Bank of England page that discusses the sandbox alongside the Synchronisation Lab, other firms are tied to sandbox activity and Quant is listed only for the non-live corporate-liquidity test.
- The current production status of Overledger Authorise after the 2023 beta described in Quant's case study.
